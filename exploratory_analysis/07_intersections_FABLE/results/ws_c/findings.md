# Workstream C: do the survey's priorities line up with what is driving the decline, and with the money?

Script: `src/ws_c_drivers.py` (Module 2 venv). All outputs in `results/ws_c/`. Everything here is exploratory and descriptive; "test" always means a Monte Carlo chi-square (20,000 permutations, seed 510) or Fisher exact, with Benjamini-Hochberg q-values within each family. Survey groups: Parent n=1,315 (Other parents 1,231; Parents who work for CHCCS 84), Non-parent n=98 (87 staff). Board n=7 from the transcribed CPA report (percentages only). Top-3 strings were parsed by matching full option labels longest-first (handles the comma inside the boundaries option and the "saftey"/"Minimize"/"Maximize" variants); the parse reproduces Module 2's published Q7 parent shares to 0.01 pt (assertion in the script). 1,398 of 1,463 responses parsed to exactly three Q7 picks and 1,408 to exactly three Q8 picks (`top3_pick_count_distribution.csv`).

## Findings

1. **The survey has no item for the thing driving two-thirds of the decline: it asks 26 questions about the district's offer, buildings and logistics, and none about how many children live here.** Mapping (`lever_mapping.csv`): DEMAND-DEMOGRAPHIC 0 items; DEMAND-CAPTURE 8 (appeal, specialty access, retaining staff, technology, excellence, student-centered, safety, meals); SUPPLY-FACILITIES/COST 13 (aging cost, safety codes, empty, overcrowded, trailers, academic fit, operational fit, boundaries, fiscal, max buildings, long-term planning, renewable, balanced populations); LOGISTICS/COMMUNITY 5 (bus commute, distance, engagement, county collaboration, partnerships). Module 1 attributes 714 of the 1,069 lost K-12 students (67%) to fewer 5-17 residents and 355 (33%) to a lower capture rate. Evidence: descriptive (a mapping, with my judgment documented per item). Caveat: "Ensuring well-balanced student populations" sits under supply per the brief but is arguably a capture item; moving it changes supply/capture shares by about 2 pts. Chart: a 26-item strip grouped by lever with the demographic lever as an empty row labeled "714 students, 0 items". Interest 4: the board's survey, by construction, cannot tell it anything about the larger driver, which argues for pairing the survey with the demographic evidence rather than reading it as a diagnosis.

2. **Every stakeholder over-attends to the capture lever and under-attends to logistics, but the board is the only group whose top-3 picks lean toward buildings and money.** Share of all top-3 picks (Q7 + Q8) landing in each lever (`headline_lever_x_stakeholder_top3_share.csv`; attention index = pick share / share of items offered, `lever_by_stakeholder_long.csv`):

   | lever (items) | Other parents | Parents who work for CHCCS | Non-parent | Board (n=7) |
   |---|---|---|---|---|
   | DEMAND-DEMOGRAPHIC (0) | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
   | DEMAND-CAPTURE (8, 31% of items) | 55.5 (idx 1.80) | 46.9 (1.52) | 46.6 (1.51) | 41.6 (1.35) |
   | SUPPLY-FACILITIES/COST (13, 50%) | 30.5 (0.61) | 42.0 (0.84) | 44.9 (0.90) | 56.0 (1.12) |
   | LOGISTICS/COMMUNITY (5, 19%) | 12.7 (0.66) | 9.3 (0.48) | 7.9 (0.41) | 2.4 (0.12) |

   Ratings tell the same story more gently: % rated 4-5 (item average) on the supply lever is 51% for other parents, 65% for parents who are staff, 69% for non-parents, 80% for the board; the board rates every lever 74-86%. Parents who work for CHCCS sit with staff, not with other parents, on all three levers (consistent with Module 2's earlier finding). Evidence: descriptive. Caveats: board percentages come from a transcribed report of seven people (one member is 14 pts); the lever shares partly reflect how many items each lever has, which is why the attention index is shown; Q7 and Q8 are pooled, and the board's Q8 picks sum to 286% rather than 300%. Chart: a slope or dot chart, four stakeholders x three levers, with a dashed "share of items offered" reference per lever. Interest 4: it says in one picture that the community is answering a different question (will families choose us?) than the one the closure process poses (what do we do with the buildings?).

3. **On the single-choice Q9 the lever gap is wider still: other parents put 79% of their one vote on the capture lever and 6% on supply; non-parent staff 62% and 28%.** Q9 share by lever (`lever_by_stakeholder_long.csv`, `q9_share`): Other parents capture 78.6 / supply 5.9 / logistics 12.2; Parents who work for CHCCS 59.5 / 30.4 / 6.3; Non-parent 61.7 / 27.7 / 9.6. Note that under the lever mapping, School safety and meal service count as capture, so this is not the same split as the four Q9 themes. Evidence: descriptive (the pre-registered Q9 test already covers the parent vs non-parent contrast). Caveat: Q9 "Other" write-ins (48) and missing (28) are excluded from the denominator only when missing. Chart: stacked 100% bars, three stakeholder groups. Interest 3: it is the same Module 2 result re-expressed in Module 1's vocabulary; useful as the bridge sentence, not as a new fact.

Educational excellence alone is in 83% of everyone's top three, which is why the any-in-top-3 indicator saturates. Evidence: exploratory tests as above. Caveats: Q8 and Q9 are not independent measurements of the same person; the 0/1/2 count test treats the few 3-pick cases as 2; non-parent CI is wide (n=98). Chart: paired dumbbells, four themes x two framings (Q8 any vs Q9 one). Data: `q8_theme_by_group.csv`. Interest 4: it reframes the headline Module 2 result; the plainer version is "staff keep learning in their top three but spend a slot on money and buildings, parents mostly do not", which is closer to what a board can act on.

5. **The Q9 gap lives among people who gave Student Learning one of their three Q8 slots, not two: with both learning values in the top three, parents and non-parents choose a learning value on Q9 at 81% vs 74%; with exactly one, 62% vs 42%.** (`q9_student_learning_conditional_on_q8.csv`, Wilson CIs: both-in-Q8 parents 80.6 (77.2-83.7, n=563) vs non-parents 74.2 (56.8-86.3, n=31); exactly-one parents 62.3 (58.5-65.9, n=668) vs non-parents 41.8 (29.7-55.0, n=55); none: 11.9 vs 8.3 (n=84, 12).) Evidence: descriptive conditional shares, no test (cells of 31 and 55). Caveat: small non-parent cells; this is a decomposition of the Q9 result, not independent evidence. Chart: three-panel bar. Interest 2: analytic nuance; one sentence at most in a narrative.

6. **At the board's own FY2027 rate of $8,356 per pupil, the 355 capture-rate students are worth about $3.0M of state funding a year and the 714 demographic students about $6.0M; over FY2027-31 at the deck's rising per-pupil rates, the capture loss alone is $16.1M, 42% of the deck's $38.3M headline.** (`decomposition_dollars.csv`.) Recomputed from `CHCCS_LAYERS`: ADM 12,335 -> 11,266, pop 5-17 13,065 -> 12,309, capture 0.9441 -> 0.9153; demographic = (pop change) x 2019 capture = 714; capture = (capture change) x 2024 pop = 355 (symmetric split: 703 / 366). Per year at FY2027 rate: total 1,069 x $8,356 = $8.9M; local funding at the FY2026 $8,895 per pupil would add $9.5M, though county appropriation is a lump sum and does not follow ADM mechanically. Cumulative FY2027-31 (per-pupil 8,356 / 8,690 / 9,038 / 9,400 / 9,776): capture $16.1M, demographic $32.3M, total $48.4M, i.e. 126% of the deck's $38.3M; the two are not the same quantity (the deck counts only ADM lost after FY2025, this counts students already gone by 2024). Evidence: arithmetic on published figures. Caveats: the dollar per pupil is an average allotment, not the marginal cost or revenue of one student; state money follows funded ADM with a one-year lag; charter, private and home school are indistinguishable in DPI data, so "capture" lumps three different reasons for leaving. Chart: a waterfall from 1,069 students to dollars with the 714 / 355 split. Data: `decomposition_dollars.csv`. Interest 5: it converts the board's own per-pupil number into the share of its deficit that is in principle recoverable (the capture third) versus not (the demographic two-thirds).

7. **The deck's $38.3M is reproducible to within $650 a year as (11,065 - prior-year ADM) x per-pupil, so the same arithmetic can be run on alternative enrollment paths: demographics-only gives $21.5M, demographics plus continued capture loss $30.7M, and the deck's own cohort projection $38.3M.** Replication (`deck_state_funding_replication.csv`): FY2027 307 ADM below FY2025 x $8,356 = $2.565M (deck $2.565M) ... FY2031 1,340 x $9,776 = $13.10M (deck $13.10M). Scenario table, anchored on the FY2025 actual 11,065 and rolled forward with SAIPE 5-17 OLS trend 2019-24 (-169/yr) and the 2019-24 capture slope (-0.0058/yr) (`scenario_table_2025_2031.csv`):

   | 2025 -> 2031, SAIPE 2019-24 window | ADM 2031 | change | vs deck 9,535 | elementary (x 0.4087) | deck-method state reduction FY27-31 |
   |---|---|---|---|---|---|
   | A: demographics only, capture held at 0.9153 | 10,141 | -924 (-8.3%) | +606 | 4,144 | $21.5M |
   | B: demographics + capture keeps falling to 0.8749 | 9,755 | -1,310 (-11.8%) | +220 | 3,987 | $30.7M |
   | C: capture restored to 0.9441 from 2026 | 10,461 | -604 (-5.5%) | +926 | 4,275 | $6.3M |
   | Board deck (cohort-based, -2.4%/yr) | 9,535 | -1,530 (-13.8%) | 0 | 3,897 | $38.3M |
   | Closure study (Carolina Demography), elementary only | | | | 4,177 | |

   With the gentler 2014-24 SAIPE window (-74/yr) the scenarios land at 10,665 / 10,259 / 11,002 and the deck is 660-1,470 students below all three. Assumptions, all stated in the script: DPI year = fiscal year (2025 = 2024-25, 11,065); SAIPE 5-17 extrapolated linearly from the 2024 value; capture rate applied to the whole 5-17 population; scenario paths anchored to the 2025 actual via year-over-year ratios (the unanchored 2025 value is 11,111 for A and 11,041 for B against the actual 11,065, so 2025 cannot tell A from B); funded ADM = prior-year ADM; per-pupil rates as in the deck; elementary scaled by the 2025 share 4,522 / 11,065. Evidence: arithmetic scenarios, no test. Caveats: the deck is cohort-based (small kindergarten classes rolling up), which a flat-capture model cannot see, and its slide says it was prepared before Carolina Demography's final data; SAIPE is a model-based estimate with sampling error of a few percent; none of this is a forecast. Chart: fan chart 2019-2031, actual ADM through 2025 then four lines (A, B, C, deck). Data: `scenario_table_2025_2031.csv` (also `adm_2026`..`adm_2030`). Interest 5: the board's financial case assumes an enrollment path steeper than demographics alone and about as steep as "capture keeps falling"; naming which assumption carries the $38.3M is the most useful thing this workstream can hand the board.

8. **No capture rate could have held 2024 enrollment at the 2019 level: it would have needed 100.2% of resident 5-17-year-olds; at the 2019 rate CHCCS would still have lost 714 students, and if the capture slide continues it costs roughly 60-70 students a year, 386 cumulative by 2031, worth $3.8M a year by FY2031.** (`task3_key_numbers.csv`: needed capture 1.0021; ADM 2024 at 2019 capture 11,621. `capture_trend_cost_by_year.csv`: B minus A = 69, 136, 202, 265, 326, 386 students in 2026-31; at that year's per-pupil $0.6M rising to $3.8M.) Evidence: arithmetic. Caveat: "capture" includes children who were never going to enroll (long-standing private-school families), so 100% is not a meaningful ceiling either; the 2019-24 capture slope is five points, not a law. Chart: a two-line chart, A vs B, with the wedge shaded and labeled in students and dollars. Interest 4: it separates the arguable part of the decline (the slide) from the unarguable part (fewer children) in units the board uses.

9. **"Appeal of the district" is the survey's capture-rate item, and the people who pick it are other parents in their 40s whose one top-of-mind value is learning; they are not the building-and-boundary people.** (`item_profiles_by_subgroup.csv`, `item_profile_tests.csv`, `item_copicks.csv`.) In top three: Other parents 25.1% (22.8-27.6), Parents who work for CHCCS 17.9% (11.1-27.4), Non-parent 12.2% (7.1-20.2); board 29%; stakeholder Monte Carlo p=0.007, q=0.012, V=0.08. By age: 40-49 26.8%, 30-39 19.5%, 50-64 18.2% (p=0.026, q=0.033, V=0.09). By Q9 theme: Student Learning 26.9%, Community 28.2% (n=39), Other write-in 31.8% (n=44), logistics 15.8%, Money & buildings 13.8% (n=58) (p=0.0003, q=0.0015, V=0.12). Language: 23.9% English vs 20.6% non-English (p=0.57). Co-picks: Appeal pickers are far less likely to also pick safety codes (25.9% vs 51.8%), overcrowded buildings (8.0% vs 27.5%) or boundaries (10.7% vs 20.2%); the only item they pick more is retaining staff (83.6% vs 79.8%). Write-ins (`item_writein_cooccurrence.csv`, 474 writers): Appeal pickers write more often (40.5% vs 31.4%) and, among writers, mention trust/transparency (20.6% vs 12.4%), housing/growth/enrollment (21.3% vs 13.0%) and safety less (7.4% vs 14.5%), but none of the 14 Fisher tests survives BH (lowest q=0.16). Evidence: exploratory tests as listed, BH within each item. Caveats: 65+ and under-30 cells are 10 and 20; "Appeal" is one word in a 13-item list and respondents may read it as reputation, not enrollment. Chart: a profile strip (age, stakeholder, Q9 theme) with Wilson bars, plus a co-pick diverging bar. Interest 3: it tells the board which constituency is already thinking about enrollment as a demand problem (a quarter of other parents) and that this group is not the one asking for boundary changes.

## Dead ends

- Language has no relationship with picking "Appeal of the district" (23.9% vs 20.6%, p=0.57).
- The Community theme (Q8 any-in-top-3) does not differ by group (16.2% vs 17.3%, p=0.78); nor does Student logistics & well-being (78.9% vs 73.5%, p=0.25).
- Write-in theme co-occurrence with "Appeal" shows suggestive but unconfirmed patterns: none of 14 Fisher tests survives BH (lowest q=0.16); same for "Retaining" (lowest q=0.19).
- "Fiscal responsibility" in Q8 top three does not differ parent vs non-parent (18.5% vs 24.5%, Fisher p=0.19), even though the 4-5 rating does; the parent/staff gap on fiscal is a rating-style gap more than a ranking gap.
- Age does nothing for "Retaining highly effective employees" (p=0.39).
- The 2025 actual ADM (11,065) cannot discriminate between demographics-only (11,111 unanchored) and continued capture loss (11,041); both are within 0.5%.
- The symmetric (Shapley) decomposition moves only 11 students between the demographic and capture components (703 / 366 vs 714 / 355); the split is not sensitive to the convention.
- Scenario C under the slow SAIPE window produces a negative "reduction" (-$6.2M), i.e. a funding gain; it is an artifact of restoring the 2019 capture rate instantly and should not be quoted.
- The closure study's per-school forecast sums to 4,184 in 2036 against the 4,183 in its text; a rounding difference, nothing more.
